# A Comprehensive Survey of Video and Multimodal Generation: Foundations, Architectures, Applications, and Future Frontiers

## TL;DR
- **Core Paradigms:** Video generation has evolved from early generative adversarial networks (GANs) and variational autoencoders (VAEs) to dominant Latent Diffusion Models (LDMs) and Diffusion Transformers (DiTs), as well as advanced autoregressive (AR) frameworks like NOVA [1][2][3].
- **Architectural Scalability:** Models such as Sora, Wan2.1, and HunyuanVideo compress video into low-dimensional latent spaces and utilize spacetime patches or block-wise causal attention to unify variable-resolution spatial-temporal generation [1][2][4][5].
- **Motion & Multimodal Control:** Trajectory-guided and multimodal conditioning techniques (e.g., Tora) integrate textual, visual, and trajectory prompts to enable precise motion synthesis and trajectory-controlled video generation [6].
- **Embodied World Models:** Generative video models increasingly serve as core world simulators and policy backbones in robotics and embodied AI (e.g., Xiaomi-Robotics-U0, WorldSimBench, EWMBench, Oneira), bridging perception, action, and open-world interaction [7][8][9][10][11].
- **Evaluation & Frontiers:** Traditional frame-level metrics (PSNR, SSIM) and distribution-based scores (FVD) face severe limitations, prompting multi-dimensional hierarchical benchmarking suites like VBench and WorldJen alongside physics-grounded diagnostic frameworks [1][12][13].

## Background
The rapid maturation of generative artificial intelligence has catalyzed a profound paradigm shift in visual media creation, propelling research from static image synthesis to high-fidelity video and multimodal generation. Historically, early video synthesis relied on conditional Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) and Recurrent Neural Network (RNN) wrappers, which suffered from severe training instability, limited temporal horizon, and poor generalization across complex motion dynamics [1]. The introduction of Latent Diffusion Models (LDMs) and Vision Transformer (ViT) backbones established a robust foundation by mapping high-dimensional video frames into compressed, low-dimensional latent spaces via efficient Video-VAEs [1][3]. 

Concurrently, Vision-Language Foundation Models (VLFMs)—including CLIP, BLIP-2, and Flamingo—have provided semantically rich, cross-modal representations that condition generation pipelines on intricate natural language prompts [3]. Modern video foundation models such as Wan2.1, HunyuanVideo, and Sora demonstrate that scaling transformer capacities alongside massive video corpora (e.g., Webvid10M) enables the synthesis of highly realistic, temporally coherent video sequences spanning diverse resolutions, aspect ratios, and durations [1][2][4][5].

## Foundations and Core Generative Paradigms
Modern video generation is anchored by two primary generative paradigms: diffusion-based models and autoregressive (AR) models [2]. Diffusion video models are further subdivided into U-Net-based architectures and Diffusion Transformer (DiT) frameworks [2]. While U-Net models proved effective for fixed-resolution generation, DiTs leverage scalable self-attention mechanisms to capture complex, long-range temporal dependencies and high-resolution spatial details across frames [2].

In parallel, autoregressive video generation has advanced through tokenized and non-quantized frameworks. For example, latent space AR models utilize discrete tokenizers—such as VQ-VAE, VQ-GAN, and codebook-free alternatives like Lookup-Free Quantization (LFQ) and Finite Scalar Quantization (FSQ)—to convert video frames into discrete tokens before applying Transformer-based sequence modeling [1]. Notably, architectures like NOVA introduce non-quantized AR video generation by employing block-wise causal masking attention and normalization layers to predict frame-by-frame temporal dynamics without quantization artifacts [2]. Text-to-video (T2V) and image-to-video (I2V) pipelines, including I2VGen-XL and VideoCrafter1, rely on multi-stage training strategies to harmonize spatial priors with temporal consistency [2].

## Advanced Architectural Innovations: Spacetime Patches and Diffusion Transformers
Scaling video generation to handle arbitrary durations, resolutions, and aspect ratios required a fundamental redesign of tokenization and backbone architectures. Pioneering models like Sora demonstrated the power of treating video as space-time patches [5]. Specifically, Sora compresses raw video into a lower-dimensional latent space both temporally and spatially, decomposing the latent representation into spacetime patches that act as scalable transformer tokens [5]. This patch-n'-pack (PNP) strategy allows the network to natively process variable lengths and spatial dimensions without mandatory center-cropping or resizing [5].

Building upon this foundation, Diffusion Transformers (DiTs) serve as the backbone for state-of-the-art video generators [2][5]. To achieve precise control over motion trajectories and complex actions, advanced frameworks such as Tora integrate specialized Trajectory Extractors (TE) with Spatial-Temporal DiT blocks [6]. By ingesting textual prompts, visual keyframes, and explicit user-drawn trajectories simultaneously, Tora guides the denoising transformer to produce motion-accurate and temporally stable video outputs [6]. Furthermore, efficient caching mechanisms, such as those in the WAN-VAE architecture, optimize memory overhead and enhance long-range temporal consistency during iterative denoising steps [4].

## Applications in Embodied AI, Robotics, and Interactive World Simulation
Generative video models have transcended passive media creation, emerging as foundational world models and interactive simulators for robotics, autonomous driving, and embodied AI [7][14][15]. Traditional physics-based simulators struggle to model complex deformable objects, fluids, and unstructured real-world interactions; in contrast, video generation models learn rich physical dynamics directly from large-scale visual data [7].

In robotic learning, video generative models serve multiple critical functions: cost-effective synthetic data generation, action prediction in imitation learning, dynamics and reward modeling in reinforcement learning, policy evaluation, and visual planning (generating step-by-step future frames for multi-stage tasks) [7]. Explicit video world models construct 3D representations—such as multi-view videos, depth maps, 4D Gaussian Splatting (e.g., Enerverse), and back-projected raymaps (e.g., Aether)—to maintain spatial memory across camera viewpoints [7]. Unified embodied architectures like Xiaomi-Robotics-U0 integrate multi-view consistency and embodied task execution into a single autoregressive framework [8].

Moreover, the paradigm of Interactive Generative Video (IGV) combines generative models with real-time user control signals [14][15]. Benchmarks such as WorldSimBench and EWMBench evaluate models from both perceptual and action-conditioned perspectives [9][10]. Frameworks like Oneira extend open-ended generation into *Open-World Interactivity*, ensuring that agent modifications to the environment persist as permanent updates to the world state rather than transient visual effects [11].

## Benchmarking, Evaluation Metrics, and Quality Assessment
Evaluating generative video models remains a formidable challenge due to the multi-dimensional nature of spatiotemporal data. Traditional reference-based metrics such as PSNR and SSIM prioritize low-level pixel fidelity over semantic correctness, while Fréchet Video Distance (FVD) heavily emphasizes distributional texture statistics rather than physical plausibility [12][13]. Furthermore, binary Visual Question Answering (VQA) benchmarks suffer from response biases and often rely on low-resolution auditors that fail to capture subtle temporal artifacts [12].

To address these limitations, hierarchical and disentangled evaluation suites have emerged. VBench structures video evaluation across 16 fine-grained dimensions spanning two coarse axes: Video Quality and Video-Condition Consistency [13]. Similarly, benchmarks like WorldJen provide end-to-end multi-dimensional assessments for generative video models [12]. Emerging diagnostic metrics—such as JEDi (JEPA Embedding Distance) and spatio-temporal semantic dependency graphs (FIFA)—offer deeper perceptual grounding, mitigating the persistent misalignment between automated metrics and human judgment observed in frameworks like TC-Bench [15].

## Trends and Open Problems
Despite remarkable strides, video and multimodal generation face significant open challenges. Computationally, extreme memory footprints and slow inference speeds necessitate resource-saving strategies such as KV caching (e.g., ViD-GPT) and distributed GPU inference (e.g., Video Infinity) [3]. Data scarcity remains a bottleneck, particularly for high-resolution, long-duration video corpora with accurate physical annotations [3].

Crucially, modern models exhibit persistent limitations in physical realism, frequently violating fundamental laws of physics such as gravity, conservation of momentum, and collision mechanics [3]. Future research directions emphasize physics-grounded benchmarks (e.g., T2VPhysBench), closed-loop agent-environment interactions, and unified video-action foundation architectures (e.g., VideoVLA, Cosmos Policy) that seamlessly couple generative video simulation with decision-making policies [14][15].
